    The Marshall lab

    Owen’s PhD and first postdoc was in Andy Choo’s group at the Murdoch Children’s Research Institute in Melbourne, working on the structure of centromeric chromatin and how it changes during cancer progression. He moved to the UK in 2012 for a second postdoc in Andrea Brand’s group at the Gurdon Institute, where he helped develop the Targeted DamID cell-type-specific profiling system, applied this to understanding the epigenetics of neural development, and learned to love living in colder weather systems. Owen returned to Australia in 2016 to start his research group at the Menzies Institute, investigating the role of epigenetics and transcription factors in brain development and disease.

    Caroline received her PhD from Kansas State University, USA, studying the role of post-translational modifications of a family of scaffolding transmembrane proteins called tetraspanins. Caroline switched to Drosophila neurobiology for her first postdoc at RIKEN, Japan, where she investigated the gene network regulating class specification in sensory neurons. Thrilled with the idea of even more systems biology, Caroline joined us for a second postdoc investigating Alzheimer’s disease and the epigenetic processes underlying memory formation in the fly brain. The course of her research may not have always run exactly smooth – but her efforts pushing the envelope of TaDa have been heroic, and we think the results are about to speak for themselves.

    Jake did Honours with us in 2020, storming through a full run of chromatin TaDa in the three weeks before covid hit, and then spending most of the year frustratingly waiting to be allowed to do lab work again. Despite being locked out of the lab for much of the year (or maybe because of it?) he went straight into a PhD with us, looking at development and disease in that “other cell type” in the brain, glia. And now he’s back for more, postdoccing his way into models of brain cancer, the SWI/SNF complex, and medium-throughput oral drug screens. Jake is blessed with eternal optimism, and science has never yet managed to break him. We hope it never does : )

    John completed a first-class Honours degree with us in 2018, working on the regulation of the transcription factor Worniu in neural stem cells. He took a break to work in the ski fields for a season, before deciding that crossing flies was even more exciting than snow boarding (it’s a close call – but yes, we agree, John). Whether it’s making new transgenics, dissecting adult fly brains like most of us shell peas, or leading his own research projects, John has golden hands that rarely if ever fail. Seven years later, I honestly don’t know how the lab would run without him (something that’s reinforced each time John takes two months leave-without-pay to indulge in his other passion, travelling :)

    Dagmara joined us to take on a PhD on how diet and stress affect a fly model of Alzheimer’s disease. Because a PhD isn’t stressful enough, she’s also decided to raise a family along the way, with three young children as well as her thesis to look after. I’m not sure how she does it, but she’s just back from her last period of maternity leave with more energy than ever. (I half wonder if Dagmara was just waiting for the rest of us to catch up with her interest in oxidative stress … if so, that might just have worked :)

    Oliver completed first-class Honours with us in 2024, took a break to briefly consider a life outside research, and then decided to dive back into the lab for a bioinformatics PhD instead. Oliver’s handling the massive data analysis side of our TF profiling, which is the other half of Fatima’s research (see below) – he’s definitely putting the work back into networks right now. Oliver has coding challenges enough of his own, but his data processing skills are prodigious and he also helps share the lab’s bioinformatics load with me – something that’s hugely appreciated :)

    We’re lucky to keep attracting great scientists who are also exceptionally talented artists to the lab, and Shiona excels at both. Shiona came for an Honours degree (first class), stayed for the PhD (there is a theme here, I’m realising), and developed a love of the SWI/SNF complex along the way. Shiona’s recently back from her first overseas conference tour and managed to pick up the travel bug en route, so I’m hoping the epigenetics of the fly brain can still compete with the allure of sitting in Eastern European cafes and sketching the local street scenes. (I think it can … right, Shiona?)

    Fatima came to us from Sheffield, UK, to start a PhD on our large neural stem cell transcription factor pokemon project (gotta profile them all!) Fatima’s pretty quiet up-front, but she has a keen sense of humour hidden away, which comes through every time she gives a presentation. She is also, thanks to her project, the host of many fly brain dissection parties (we really must get that mirror ball for the lab). Fatima’s taken the TaDa profiling of several hundred TFs in her stride, which is honestly quite the superpower :)

    Osian had his “come to Drosophila” moment with only one and a half years left on the PhD clock, after his initial PhD research hit a wall. (Mammals are seriously problematic disease models at times …) When Osian isn’t looking at the effects of microplastics on neurodegenerative disease, he’s making more of them by designing and 3D printing half the lab’s wetware. Somehow, he’s managing to compress a full PhD’s worth of research into half a PhD’s worth of time, which is seriously impressive stuff.
